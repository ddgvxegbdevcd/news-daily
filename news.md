## The political ad wars are still being fought over the airwaves even as digital gains share

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/21/why-broadcast-tv-still-dominates-political-ad-spending.html

正文长度：9190

摘要：Broadcast TV is set to capture nearly half of political advertising spending ahead of the midterm election as local stations benefit from record spending.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> While advertising dollars increasingly shift toward streaming and digital platforms, the political ad war is still being fought over the free airwaves.
Broadcast television companies are set to capture the greatest portion of political ad spend ahead of the midterm elections, according to advertising intelligence company AdImpact.
The 2025-26 election cycle is forecast to be the most expensive on record, with a predicted $11.6 billion in total spending. That's higher than the $11.2 billion spent on advertising during the 2024 presidential election cycle and the $8.9 billion spent during the 2022 midterms.
An estimated $5.6 billion of advertising spend ahead of the midterms, or 48%, is expected to go to broadcast companies, AdImpact found.
"It's still a local game," said StoneX Equity Research Analyst Daniel Kurnos in an interview. "Yes, there are national elections, but you have to win counties. And if you have to win counties, you have to reach voters in these counties."
Broadcast audiences — those tuning into NBC, ABC, CBS, Fox or their local affiliate stations — are generally more politically active, Kurnos said: "You cater to the demos that vote. You cater to the votes that matter."
That makes political advertising one of the last bastions of broadcast's dominance.
While broadcasters are expected to capture roughly half of political advertising spending this cycle, they account for just 7.7% of the more than $470 billion in general advertising spend forecast for this year in the United States, according to Magna data published by S&P Global in July.
Reach, demographics and trust
Terry Nelson, a founding partner of Plus Communications and the national political director for former President George W. Bush's 2004 reelection campaign, told CNBC that broadcast spots allow political strategists to reach large numbers of voters "as efficiently as we can from a cost perspective."
Though cord-cutting has reshaped the TV landscape as customers drop cable, broadcast remains free and broadly accessible.
Broadcast, which transmits local news, sports, classic game shows like "Wheel of Fortune" and national networks, captured more hours of television use than cable in July, according to Nielsen's most recent monthly streaming report, The Gauge. The category accounted for 19.5% of television use, while cable captured 18.7%. Streaming led television use by far, at 49%.
Broadcast is still particularly good at delivering high viewership during live sports games, Nelson noted.
"If you think about an NFL audience, for example, you're still going to deliver big audiences amongst large segments of the population," he said. "So no matter how you dice that demographic, whether it's men, women, or old people, young people, or whatever the case might be, the NFL game is still going to deliver large segments of all of those audiences."
Brad Seitter, CEO of TVB, a trade organization representing the local broadcast industry, boiled down broadcast's strength during election cycles to "trust, reach and because it works."
Seitter told CNBC in a statement that television's perceived reliability is of utmost importance for political campaigns.
"Gaining the trust of a voter equals a vote for your candidate or issue," Seitter said.
He also said that he believes broadcast does a better job of promoting political candidates and issues than some digital channels.
"You may see a political ad on social media, but you don't trust the content of the ad because users are conditioned to not trust what they hear or see on social media," Seitter said. "Conversely, viewers trust their local broadcast TV news, so when they view a political ad, they are more likely to believe the content of the ad on a platform they know has long-standing rules, regulations and trust."
Strategic prowess or muscle memory?
Seitter said that political campaigns continue to place advertisements on traditional TV because there is clear evidence that they are influential on voting behavior.
"Nothing moves the needle in polling like running an ad on broadcast TV," Seitter said.
According to a study conducted by TVB, 42% of Democrats and 49% of Republicans said broadcast or cable television was the most important form of advertising media in influencing how they cast their vote.
Erik Brydges, head of performance and political demand at sell-side ad company Magnite, told CNBC that broadcast's perceived influence is tied to the long history of the platform.
"There are multiple studies out there that show a correlation between the number of points you buy on TV in relation to moving poll numbers, because the medium's been around for like 70 years," he said.
And yet Brydges said he believes that broadcasting's strength is also due, in part, to muscle memory.
"A lot of campaigns and a lot of the consultants who work on the campaigns have just been really used to buying TV," Brydges said. "There's an old adage out there that no political consultant is ever going to get fired for suggesting buying more TV."
Winning the midterms
Broadcast companies are already feeling the windfall from elevated advertising spending.
Publicly traded broadcasters, including Nexstar Media Group, Gray Media, Sinclair and E.W. Scripps, each reported high political advertising revenue in their most recent fiscal quarters and forecast continued growth through the fall as the midterms inch closer.
StoneX's Kurnos told CNBC that broadcasting companies rely on the low-lift revenue from political advertising to help manage their debt.
"They have all the infrastructure in place for [standard advertising] already," Kurnos said. "And you don't need to work on the creative. You don't need to work on the selling. … Political is just, therefore, pure margin dollars."
Nexstar, which owns or operates at least 265 stations and a slew of websites and mobile apps, reported its highest quarterly revenue in the company's history. Net revenue hit $1.99 billion, and advertising revenue was $862 million, according to the August earnings report.
Overall revenue in the second fiscal quarter was largely driven by the incorporation of broadcast rival Tegna, which Nexstar officially acquired in March.
However, even discounting additional revenue from the merger, the company still reported fiscal second-quarter growth at legacy Nexstar that was primarily driven by $75 million of political advertising revenue, according to the company's most recent earnings call.
Gray Media, though operationally smaller than Nexstar, could also be a company to watch, according to Kurnos.
The company's stations operate in several markets with the most competitive races and thus the greatest ad spend: Gray has exposure in 12 competitive Senate races and all 11 competitive gubernatorial races, according to Kurnos.
"Gray has always been the most efficient political generator of the broadcast group," he said.
Sinclair and Scripps also reported political ad revenue as a bright spot in their earnings reports for the most recent fiscal quarter.
E.W. Scripps said that political ad revenue hit the highest second-quarter level ever, while Sinclair said that political ad revenue had grown 9% from the second quarter of 2022 during the last midterms season.
"No other medium delivers a political message as powerfully and reliably as broadcast television," E.W. Scripps CEO Adam Symson said on the company's second-quarter earnings call.
The next frontier: Connected TV
On the other end of the media spectrum, connected TV represents the fastest-growing category of political advertising spend, according to AdImpact. The category encompasses internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku.
It's predicted to capture 23%, or $2.7 billion, of this cycle's ad spending, AdImpact found, and is likely to continue to gain share.
Magnite's Brydges said the complicated and rapidly changing nature of connected TV, or CTV, and free, ad-supported streaming channels — also called FAST — has prevented some advertisers thus far from taking advantage of high viewership numbers.
"If you look at some of the FAST streaming services — some of your Plutos, your Tubis — a campaign or a consultant might not understand what those are," Brydges said. "Therefore, they might just be like, 'Well, if I don't understand what it is, then it's probably not worth investment.'"
However, he said he believes political consultants are becoming more attuned to the benefits that these platforms provide.
SKDK's Pia Carusone, who manages the firm's political advertising department, told CNBC in an interview that she sees value in streaming's specific audience targeting features.
"If you're buying NBC in New York City, for example, you're buying the whole market," she said of traditional advertising strategy. "Increasingly, now with the streaming and CTV platforms, they're able to really offer a lot more nuance in their targeting. So we're able to sort of deliver against those audiences more directly."
But ultimately, Carusone said, political advertising strategy relies on maintaining a diverse portfolio.
"You can't just do digital. You can't just do linear," she said. "Good buyers today are buying across the spectrum."

## Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley are dominating the AI stock boom. Here's their edge

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/21/goldman-sachs-jpmorgan-morgan-stanley-ai-trading.html

正文长度：7960

摘要：Wall Street’s Big Three have generated $52 billion in equity trading revenue this year, capturing about 80% of the market on rising margin lending to hedge funds.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Fueled by the global boom in artificial intelligence stocks, relaxed capital rules and greater lending to hedge funds, big Wall Street banks have already had a banner year for equities trading — even with one quarter left to go.
The five biggest investment banks have generated about $67 billion in equities revenue through Sept. 30, according to company filings. That is $7 billion more than what the group made during all of 2025, which itself was a record haul.
But it's Wall Street's Big Three that have captured the largest slice of what is shaping up to be the industry's most lucrative arena: Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase and Morgan Stanley have made a combined $52 billion in stock trading revenue so far this year, nearly 80% of the total.
"When it comes to the AI mega trade, the Big Three consider each other their main competitors, and that's pretty much it," Mike Mayo, Wells Fargo banking analyst, told CNBC. "They've been best positioned to take advantage of this market."
Wall Street banks are experiencing an epic run of blockbuster profits as the total value of the stock market balloons and trading activity from institutional and retail investors rises across stocks, exchange-traded funds and derivatives. Helping drive this activity are the loans that the banks, through their prime brokerages, are extending to hedge funds so that they can amplify their bets.
Prime brokerage — the business of providing hedge funds with margin loans, securities lending and other trading services — has become a key battleground and source of growth. The Big Three's prime brokers are central to the larger equities story because they serve the largest funds across markets and products.
Demand for leverage to make bets on AI hardware, hyperscalers and chip suppliers has reached new heights, bank executives said last week.
At Goldman Sachs, equities financing activities jumped 96% in the third quarter to a record $3.37 billion. JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley don't break out that figure, but said they saw a similar dynamic.
"I like your 'insatiable demand for capital' point. That feels about right," JPMorgan CFO Jeremy Barnum said on the bank's third-quarter conference call, responding to analyst questions about financing demands.
"In this moment where AI is such an overwhelming theme ... there is capital being tapped through all kinds of different mechanisms," Barnum said.
That demand has helped transform equity desks at the Big Three from cyclical, transactional businesses into more durable, high-margin lending engines, Wall Street executives say.
The performance gap between Wall Street's giants and rivals including Bank of America and Citigroup highlights a widening divide in capital markets.
"For clients, the value isn't just a price on one trade, it's coverage and continuity," Rachid Alaoui, JPMorgan global head of equities, told CNBC in an interview. "You want a dealer that can give you the full suite across products, and you want that service to travel with you across regions. That matters in stressed markets and periods of volatility."
Executing multibillion-dollar bets, like leveraged swaps on volatile AI stocks or sector ETFs, can require an enormous balance sheet. The biggest banks can absorb potential $100 million losses without threatening their quarters, according to trading executives. Smaller or more niche banks can't underwrite single-trade risks of that magnitude, forcing them to decline the business or cede share.
U.S. prime brokers have also benefited from the retreat of European banks including Credit Suisse from the equities business, said Bloomberg Intelligence analyst Herman Chan.
"Prime is one of the ultimate scale businesses," Chan said. "If you have a strong equities franchise, the hedge funds will want to transact with you because you have a bigger pool of trading opportunities with counterparties."
Picks and shovels
While U.S. markets were swept up in software and cloud computing giants this year, trading executives say the fastest-growing frontier for equities was in Asia.
Institutional investors poured into South Korea and Taiwan, targeting the "picks and shovels" of the AI supply chain: the memory chipmakers and semiconductor manufacturers supporting data center construction, Alaoui said.
For the Big Three, with their footprints across Tokyo; Hong Kong; Seoul, South Korea; and Mumbai, India; the Asia trade was a goldmine. However, trading executives point out that transaction taxes and stamp duties are included in top-line revenue, artificially inflating Asia prime totals.
There is also no agreed-upon definition of what equities trading revenue should include, so some banks include business lines like clearing services that others exclude from their stock trading totals, making apples-to-apples comparisons difficult.
But that doesn't change the overall momentum of the business, boosted by a stock rally that began in October 2022, weeks before OpenAI released ChatGPT.
"We're still in a stock-based world," Morgan Stanley CEO Ted Pick said on the firm's earnings call. "Equities are still, all things being equal, the attractive asset class."
While the CEOs of Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley have said the AI build-out is still in its early years, if AI turns out to be not as transformative as hoped, a downturn could impact those banks more than others, analysts said.
The boom has also drawn some concern over the possible risks posed by hedge funds.
The International Monetary Fund warned this month that growing leverage, crowded positions and correlated selling from hedge funds could create potential stability risks. The IMF estimated that global hedge fund assets had reached $13 trillion.
In August, JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon said industrywide leverage was "the highest it's ever been," though he stopped short of calling the risk big enough to pose a threat to the financial markets.
Wall Street executives and analysts pointed out that the industry wasn't fazed by the meltdown of several hedge funds this summer. While the hedge fund run by former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner, Situational Awareness, garnered the most headlines, several smaller firms also experienced a forced unwinding.
Prime brokers have learned to leap on margin calls and quickly take down risk when markets move against clients, JPMorgan's Alaoui said.
"In the summer, some investors had positions they were forced to unwind," he told CNBC. "They deleveraged, risk came down, and the market moved on."
'Known unknowns'
Another factor driving the equities boom: Trump administration regulators have eased some leverage rules for the largest U.S. banks, giving them more room to expand balance sheet-heavy businesses including prime brokerage. The revised rules were finalized in 2025, and banks were allowed to adopt them as early as January.
By easing the stringent balance sheet caps that previously treated Treasurys and prime brokerage loans, which are collateralized, the same as riskier assets, regulators gave dealers greater flexibility to deploy balance sheet capital.
"Capital did go down, so you have to say the system is somewhat more levered because of the relaxation in regulations," said Darrell Duffie, a finance professor at Stanford University and a leading expert on financial stability. "It's not a good idea, but it hasn't gotten dramatically out of whack."
The larger size of the market and higher volatility can create more trading, derivatives activity and leverage, he said, but that's not inherently alarming.
Wall Street executives also downplayed concerns over leverage, pointing out that they use collateral and frequent stress testing to get ahead of problems.
"Hedge fund leverage as a contributor of risk to the system is very much in the category of known unknowns," JPMorgan's Barnum said last week. "It doesn't mean it's riskless, but we kind of know what it is."
— CNBC's Leslie Picker and Ritika Shah contributed to this report.
