The carrier expects to earn between $2.60 and $2.85 a share on an adjusted basis this year, down from its forecast in July of between $3.25 to as much as $4.25 a share.
Here's what Southwest reported for third quarter compared with Wall Street expectations, according to consensus estimates from LSEG:
- Earnings per share: 66 cents adjusted vs. 58 cents expected
- Revenue: $8.42 billion adjusted vs. $8.25 billion expected

## Netflix has dipped its toe into theatrical. Is it ready to jump in?

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/21/netflix-theatrical-strategy.html

正文长度：8485

摘要：Co-CEO Ted Sarandos has said the streamer needs new avenues for growth and to improve execution around its content strategy.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Netflix is making itself clear: It's a streaming-first business.
During the company's third-quarter earnings call on Tuesday, co-CEO Ted Sarandos reiterated the streaming giant's long-held stance that its content, which it spent $20 billion on last year, is meant for its subscribers and, therefore, should be delivered to customers on its streaming platform as quickly as possible.
Yet, the company is set to have 30 theatrical releases in 2027, on par with what it brought to cinemas in 2026. And while not all of these features are expected to have wide releases or long runs in theaters, Netflix is testing the waters with projects like Greta Gerwig's "Narnia: The Magician's Nephew," which will have a wide release and a 49-day exclusive theatrical window when it hits cinemas in February.
The company seems poised to carve a new path forward as it grapples with viewership stagnation and a stock slump of roughly 30% since January.
How its theatrical ambitions fit into a streaming-first strategy remains a question mark.
"Theatrical releases are a net positive for reach and streaming demand," Alicia Reese, analyst at Wedbush, wrote in a note Wednesday. "Netflix's more amenable attitude toward exclusive theatrical windows will not only facilitate better relationships with talent, but also add a new revenue stream and build interest in its streaming service. This follows the pattern we have seen with the major studios' streaming platforms over the past several years: theatrical releases don't cannibalize the streaming window, as Netflix fears."
Ahead of its Tuesday earnings report, Netflix restructured its C-suite, bringing in former Disney executive Peter Rice to take over as the company's chief content officer, ousting 10-year veteran Bela Bajaria.
Netflix also dismissed Dan Lin, the company's head of film, according to a person familiar with the matter who spoke on the condition of anonymity to discuss internal changes. That came just days after it laid off roughly 1,000 employees including Lin's right hand Doug Belgrad as well as Sheroum Kim, the director of independent film, according to various media reports. It's unclear who Netflix will tap to take over Lin's position.
The company aims to restore quality to the brand and utilize Rice's expertise in talent relations to lure big names in an increasingly competitive market.
Still, Netflix executives seemed to downplay the change in leadership during the company's earnings call.
"Our business and our business flywheel are working," Sarandos told investors during the company's earnings call. "There is no need for a fundamental shift in the strategy. We do need better execution in the U.S. ... It's just not growing as fast as it could or as fast as we'd like."
Sarandos said the streamer had "some really solid doubles and triples this year," but suggested the company needs more "home runs."
In an emblematic moment during Tuesday's call, Sarandos highlighted a deal with Disney, struck in October, to bring the Ice Age films and others to the streaming service, and gave a nod to that company's CEO, Josh D'Amaro.
"We already are seeing some of those benefits from the Ice Age movies. All five of them hit our top 10. That's really exciting," the Netflix co-CEO said. "And by the way, yes, Netflix just plugged a Disney movie on our earnings call. You're welcome, Josh."
Netflix's content strategy
In a bid to capture consumer attention and broaden its subscriber funnel, Netflix has spent years diversifying its offerings to include video games, live sports and quick-turn documentaries on buzzy cultural moments.
But more recently, it's lacked some of the water-cooler-type hits like "Stranger Things," "Bridgerton" and "Squid Game" that previously boosted subscriptions and viewership numbers.
"We know there's some investor concern about our view hours growth," Netflix said in its shareholder letter Tuesday. "Engagement — which encompasses quantity, quality and variety of viewing — remains healthy."
And yet with Netflix stating plainly that it needs new avenues for growth — and against the backdrop of a foiled deal to buy the Warner Bros. film studio — investors and onlookers are once again looking at the theatrical business.
"I think there's a place for the theatrical for Netflix," Matthew Condon, equity research analyst at Citizens, told CNBC. "Especially, as the competition for time and attention just continues to increase."
"If they really want to build stickier fan bases around some of their [intellectual property], I think theatrical could be a good avenue for them to do that," Condon said. "That's one area that Netflix has historically not been all that strong in, is building like really, really strong IP. And I think building fan bases around theatrical releases and things of that nature can help with that and then also can help them get top tier talent."
Netflix is no stranger to luring in big Hollywood directors. It's worked with the likes of Rian Johnson, Martin Scorsese, Alfonso Cuarón, Bong Joon Ho, Spike Lee and Guillermo del Toro. However, none of these directors' Netflix films received a wide theatrical release or a long run in theaters.
Sarandos admitted earlier this month that the company has lost talent because it would not guarantee wide theatrical releases.
Most notably, the creators and lead producer behind its hit "Stranger Things" have all jumped ship in the last year. Matt and Ross Duffer signed a four-year exclusive deal with Skydance — the new home of the same Warner Bros. film studio that Netflix nearly bought — while Shawn Levy joined the ranks of Disney to direct "Star Wars: Starfighter."
Going to the movies
Sarandos said Tuesday the company's strategy is about "specialty theatrical releasing," in which each film gets its own bespoke release. In some cases, Netflix plays the film in theaters for the minimum requirement to be eligible for awards. Others appear for weekend stints as one-off events, like in the case of its "KPop Demon Hunters" smash that ran in theaters for just two days.
Gerwig's "Narnia" adaptation is among those chosen few to get longer theatrical runs and a wider reach, along with an animated film called "Charlie vs. the Chocolate Factory."
"Both films have big global IP and big global fandom, so we're very excited about how they'll play out next year," Sarandos said.
In another sign of its evolution into theaters, Netflix has said it will report box office results for films that have longer theatrical windows. Those readouts are standard for major studios, regardless of release or windowing, but the move marks a new level of disclosure for the streamer.
Incoming content head Rice could be the missing piece for Netflix in a new theatrical model.
The executive has a wealth of experience in television and film. Since 2023 he has produced films like "Saturday Night" and "28 Years Later." He's currently developing a movie adaptation of the video game "Elden Ring."
But right now, Netflix doesn't have the infrastructure for a full theatrical commitment.
"Going into theatrical isn't quite as easy as I think sometimes folks make it sound," Steven Cahall, stock analyst at Wells Fargo, told CNBC. "You have to negotiate with movie theaters, and find spots for your films."
Cahall noted that sending the occasional film to theaters, especially with a limited release, "maybe isn't so hard, but there is a muscle of theatrical distribution ... You need a team of people, and you need a pipeline of content that's intended for theatrical release, and you need a marketing department around it."
That's where buying Warner Bros. Discovery's studio assets would have come in. In the absence of that acquisition, it's not clear how, or how quickly, Netflix can bulk up its own operations.
Plus, it's previously balked at the marketing costs that come with bringing films to the cinema, which can total hundreds of millions of dollars per film. Despite a hefty content budget, Netflix has long prioritized quantity and seemingly endless choice for its subscribers over individual big-budget projects.
A shift to a multi-pronged strategy that feeds both streaming and cinemas would require Netflix to more closely mimic the companies it disrupted in the first place.
"So that becomes more of an existential question for Netflix: Is it time to become a media conglomerate rather than being a pure play streaming company?" Wells Fargo's Cahall said. "'Do we want to become a media conglomerate?'"

## GM aims to develop vehicles in as little as two years with AI, software push

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/21/gm-vehicle-development-ai-software.html

正文长度：6663

摘要：General Motors is targeting a roughly two-year vehicle development cycle as it expands its use of artificial intelligence, simulation and vehicle software.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> NEW YORK — General Motors plans to lean into artificial intelligence and vehicle software for future growth and development amid increasing competition abroad from Chinese automakers and a tougher U.S. market.
The Detroit automaker on Wednesday shared new details about a proprietary AI agent it's releasing under its OnStar brand, plans for a fourth generation of more efficient all-electric vehicles and a target to cut its vehicle development time in half.
GM also reconfirmed that by 2028, it wants to have eyes-off highway driving, or "personal autonomy," that will let drivers take their attention off the road. The automaker said that technology would start with the Cadillac Escalade IQ EV before rolling out to other, more high-volume models.
"All of these developments are building us the car of the future," CEO Mary Barra said Wednesday during an event laying out the plans, called "GM Forward," in downtown Manhattan.
The initiatives, which Barra, Chief Product Officer Sterling Anderson and other executives discussed at the event, are designed to streamline the automaker's operations, while also improving the capabilities of its vehicles for customers.
"Over the last 12 months, we've evolved how we design, engineer and build our vehicles," said Anderson, who led the event and who has been tasked with overseeing GM's latest technology transformation.
GM said it is now targeting a vehicle development time of roughly two years from concept to production versus a traditional four- to five-year cycle. However, the company did not detail a timeline for reaching that goal.
The event comes as Chinese automakers are growing outside of the U.S. with rapid development times of new technologies and vehicles. Consulting firm AlixPartners said it believes China is currently bringing vehicles to market twice as fast as traditional automakers, with 40% to 50% less investment and a 30% cost advantage.
GM also continues to play catch-up to U.S. EV leader Tesla when it comes to delivering technology advancements through over-the-air updates and automated driving capabilities.
Anderson, a former Tesla executive, declined to speak specifically about how GM's technologies will compare to the EV maker, but said GM's systems will be able to visualize, or see, more things through the use of multiple types of sensors than a strictly camera-based system, which Tesla uses.
He also said the rapid development and new software architecture will help the company better compete against Chinese automakers abroad.
"There's inherent virtue to speed," Anderson told CNBC during an interview after the event. "Not just in being able to get a car out faster, it's being able to develop a system that's far more performant that you could have otherwise."
Next-generation vehicles
GM said Wednesday it has major plans for its next generation of EVs and traditional vehicles with internal combustion engines.
The company said those coming vehicles — regardless of type — will have 40 times the computing power and up to 50% faster cellular connectivity than prior systems.
That computing power is expected to help the automaker with over-the-air updates, onboard diagnostics and AI, as well as other technologies such as automated driving features.
The new brain of the vehicle will launch on the $130,000 Cadillac Escalade IQ EV in 2028 before rolling out to other models, including its next generation of EVs.
GM said the forthcoming EVs are expected to be 25% more aerodynamic and 15% lighter and feature electric motors and battery packs that are more compact.
GM President Mark Reuss said despite lackluster consumer adoption and the automaker pulling back billions of dollars in investments in EVs, it remains committed to producing them.
"We continue to develop our future EVs and work to reduce battery costs," Reuss said during the event. "And when the U.S. market is ready for EVs in large numbers, we will be there for them."
AI development
GM expects to achieve such targets through the use of AI in research and development.
The company said it is developing a "new operating approach for vehicle development" that leans into enterprise AI and virtualization to increase speed.
"This concurrent engineering model allows us to innovate faster, improve continuously, and scale new technologies across our lineup with incredible agility," the company said in a release.
GM used the development of its next-generation vehicles, including a new battery chemistry, as an example of how the new technologies are reshaping its vehicle development.
The company said it virtually tested more than 300,000 battery designs using its recently announced lithium manganese rich battery chemistry, or LMR, before building a single physical prototype.
Anderson said Wednesday that LMR will be the exclusive battery tech for its fourth-generation EVs. GM had previously said it wanted to use those batteries in full-size electric vehicles such as its Chevrolet Silverado and Escalade IQ beginning in 2028.
Anderson said GM is uniquely positioned to use AI compared to other automotive companies because of its more than 100 years in the industry as well as 30 years of OnStar.
"In the age of AI, data is currency," Anderson said. "For decades, GM's engineering, manufacturing and product teams have generated unparalleled body of data that we're now using to build industry-leading AI tools."
In-vehicle AI
The company is building on its internal use of AI to better shape its experiences for customers.
GM on Wednesday reconfirmed that it plans to launch a proprietary in-vehicle AI assistant through its OnStar technology brand.
The OnStar AI assistant is expected to launch in the coming months on the 2027 Cadillac Lyriq EV through an over-the-air update before expanding to other models.
GM currently uses Google's Gemini for its in-vehicle AI assistant. The company told CNBC earlier this year that the GM-developed AI will be more integrated with the vehicle.
The goal for OnStar AI, which will be available through a subscription, is to help customers be productive while in the car, especially if the vehicle has self-driving capabilities that don't require the driver or passengers to monitor the system.
GM on Wednesday also reiterated its plans to launch eyes-off highway driving in 2028. The automaker said a year ago that it wanted to launch that technology. It's been five years since Barra said she expected the automaker to offer personal autonomous vehicles this decade.
"Our vision is that eventually every vehicle should drive itself or its occupants anywhere, and we're investing across the self-driving stack to make that real," Anderson said.

## The political ad wars are still being fought over the airwaves even as digital gains share

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/21/why-broadcast-tv-still-dominates-political-ad-spending.html

正文长度：9190

摘要：Broadcast TV is set to capture nearly half of political advertising spending ahead of the midterm election as local stations benefit from record spending.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> While advertising dollars increasingly shift toward streaming and digital platforms, the political ad war is still being fought over the free airwaves.
Broadcast television companies are set to capture the greatest portion of political ad spend ahead of the midterm elections, according to advertising intelligence company AdImpact.
The 2025-26 election cycle is forecast to be the most expensive on record, with a predicted $11.6 billion in total spending. That's higher than the $11.2 billion spent on advertising during the 2024 presidential election cycle and the $8.9 billion spent during the 2022 midterms.
An estimated $5.6 billion of advertising spend ahead of the midterms, or 48%, is expected to go to broadcast companies, AdImpact found.
"It's still a local game," said StoneX Equity Research Analyst Daniel Kurnos in an interview. "Yes, there are national elections, but you have to win counties. And if you have to win counties, you have to reach voters in these counties."
Broadcast audiences — those tuning into NBC, ABC, CBS, Fox or their local affiliate stations — are generally more politically active, Kurnos said: "You cater to the demos that vote. You cater to the votes that matter."
That makes political advertising one of the last bastions of broadcast's dominance.
While broadcasters are expected to capture roughly half of political advertising spending this cycle, they account for just 7.7% of the more than $470 billion in general advertising spend forecast for this year in the United States, according to Magna data published by S&P Global in July.
Reach, demographics and trust
Terry Nelson, a founding partner of Plus Communications and the national political director for former President George W. Bush's 2004 reelection campaign, told CNBC that broadcast spots allow political strategists to reach large numbers of voters "as efficiently as we can from a cost perspective."
Though cord-cutting has reshaped the TV landscape as customers drop cable, broadcast remains free and broadly accessible.
Broadcast, which transmits local news, sports, classic game shows like "Wheel of Fortune" and national networks, captured more hours of television use than cable in July, according to Nielsen's most recent monthly streaming report, The Gauge. The category accounted for 19.5% of television use, while cable captured 18.7%. Streaming led television use by far, at 49%.
Broadcast is still particularly good at delivering high viewership during live sports games, Nelson noted.
"If you think about an NFL audience, for example, you're still going to deliver big audiences amongst large segments of the population," he said. "So no matter how you dice that demographic, whether it's men, women, or old people, young people, or whatever the case might be, the NFL game is still going to deliver large segments of all of those audiences."
Brad Seitter, CEO of TVB, a trade organization representing the local broadcast industry, boiled down broadcast's strength during election cycles to "trust, reach and because it works."
Seitter told CNBC in a statement that television's perceived reliability is of utmost importance for political campaigns.
"Gaining the trust of a voter equals a vote for your candidate or issue," Seitter said.
He also said that he believes broadcast does a better job of promoting political candidates and issues than some digital channels.
"You may see a political ad on social media, but you don't trust the content of the ad because users are conditioned to not trust what they hear or see on social media," Seitter said. "Conversely, viewers trust their local broadcast TV news, so when they view a political ad, they are more likely to believe the content of the ad on a platform they know has long-standing rules, regulations and trust."
Strategic prowess or muscle memory?
Seitter said that political campaigns continue to place advertisements on traditional TV because there is clear evidence that they are influential on voting behavior.
"Nothing moves the needle in polling like running an ad on broadcast TV," Seitter said.
According to a study conducted by TVB, 42% of Democrats and 49% of Republicans said broadcast or cable television was the most important form of advertising media in influencing how they cast their vote.
Erik Brydges, head of performance and political demand at sell-side ad company Magnite, told CNBC that broadcast's perceived influence is tied to the long history of the platform.
"There are multiple studies out there that show a correlation between the number of points you buy on TV in relation to moving poll numbers, because the medium's been around for like 70 years," he said.
And yet Brydges said he believes that broadcasting's strength is also due, in part, to muscle memory.
"A lot of campaigns and a lot of the consultants who work on the campaigns have just been really used to buying TV," Brydges said. "There's an old adage out there that no political consultant is ever going to get fired for suggesting buying more TV."
Winning the midterms
Broadcast companies are already feeling the windfall from elevated advertising spending.
Publicly traded broadcasters, including Nexstar Media Group, Gray Media, Sinclair and E.W. Scripps, each reported high political advertising revenue in their most recent fiscal quarters and forecast continued growth through the fall as the midterms inch closer.
StoneX's Kurnos told CNBC that broadcasting companies rely on the low-lift revenue from political advertising to help manage their debt.
"They have all the infrastructure in place for [standard advertising] already," Kurnos said. "And you don't need to work on the creative. You don't need to work on the selling. … Political is just, therefore, pure margin dollars."
Nexstar, which owns or operates at least 265 stations and a slew of websites and mobile apps, reported its highest quarterly revenue in the company's history. Net revenue hit $1.99 billion, and advertising revenue was $862 million, according to the August earnings report.
Overall revenue in the second fiscal quarter was largely driven by the incorporation of broadcast rival Tegna, which Nexstar officially acquired in March.
However, even discounting additional revenue from the merger, the company still reported fiscal second-quarter growth at legacy Nexstar that was primarily driven by $75 million of political advertising revenue, according to the company's most recent earnings call.
Gray Media, though operationally smaller than Nexstar, could also be a company to watch, according to Kurnos.
The company's stations operate in several markets with the most competitive races and thus the greatest ad spend: Gray has exposure in 12 competitive Senate races and all 11 competitive gubernatorial races, according to Kurnos.
"Gray has always been the most efficient political generator of the broadcast group," he said.
Sinclair and Scripps also reported political ad revenue as a bright spot in their earnings reports for the most recent fiscal quarter.
E.W. Scripps said that political ad revenue hit the highest second-quarter level ever, while Sinclair said that political ad revenue had grown 9% from the second quarter of 2022 during the last midterms season.
"No other medium delivers a political message as powerfully and reliably as broadcast television," E.W. Scripps CEO Adam Symson said on the company's second-quarter earnings call.
The next frontier: Connected TV
On the other end of the media spectrum, connected TV represents the fastest-growing category of political advertising spend, according to AdImpact. The category encompasses internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku.
It's predicted to capture 23%, or $2.7 billion, of this cycle's ad spending, AdImpact found, and is likely to continue to gain share.
Magnite's Brydges said the complicated and rapidly changing nature of connected TV, or CTV, and free, ad-supported streaming channels — also called FAST — has prevented some advertisers thus far from taking advantage of high viewership numbers.
"If you look at some of the FAST streaming services — some of your Plutos, your Tubis — a campaign or a consultant might not understand what those are," Brydges said. "Therefore, they might just be like, 'Well, if I don't understand what it is, then it's probably not worth investment.'"
However, he said he believes political consultants are becoming more attuned to the benefits that these platforms provide.
SKDK's Pia Carusone, who manages the firm's political advertising department, told CNBC in an interview that she sees value in streaming's specific audience targeting features.
"If you're buying NBC in New York City, for example, you're buying the whole market," she said of traditional advertising strategy. "Increasingly, now with the streaming and CTV platforms, they're able to really offer a lot more nuance in their targeting. So we're able to sort of deliver against those audiences more directly."
But ultimately, Carusone said, political advertising strategy relies on maintaining a diverse portfolio.
"You can't just do digital. You can't just do linear," she said. "Good buyers today are buying across the spectrum."
