## Cruise lines chase luxury travelers with suites for up to $25,000 a night

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/22/luxury-cruise-suites-regent-seven-seas.html

正文长度：5352

摘要：CNBC got a tour of the Regent Suite, a top-of-the-line offering on Regent's Seven Seas Splendor that goes for $15,000 a night.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> The latest push into high-end travel is taking place on the high seas, as cruise lines increasingly lean on luxury offerings.
On Regent's Seven Seas Splendor, an all-luxury ship, cabins cost anywhere from $1,800 to $15,000 per night for its top-of-the-line booking, the Regent Suite.
The difference is not just the roughly 5,100 square feet of space. The top-tier suite has a Steinway piano, a private dining room and a wraparound deck with a hot tub and a view comparable to the one from the ship's bridge.
Multiple passengers wanted the suite for the Splendor’s 151-night global cruise that embarks in January, Regent told CNBC, and it is almost always occupied.
Regent is setting its sights even higher, as the cruise line plans to debut the Seven Seas Prestige in December, its first new ship class in a decade. Its top suite will cost $25,000 per night.
While megaships with surfing and waterslides can carry more than 7,500 passengers, the smaller, elite ships — some carrying fewer than a thousand passengers — are powering profits for companies like Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings. The company owns luxury lines Regent and Oceania.
Norwegian CEO John Chidsey has said he's focused on sharpening the luxury brands' positioning, improving the products and guest experience, and strengthening their commercial performance.
Regent and Oceania, he said, are in good shape for 2027, though Chidsey did not break out metrics between the companies.
Cruise competitors are also betting big on luxury offerings, investing in new ships and more specialized experiences.
Regent competes with established luxury cruise lines including Royal Caribbean's Silversea and Carnival's Seabourn, as well as newer entrants carrying hotel names into the market. Four Seasons, Aman and Orient Express are among the brands bringing luxury hospitality to yacht-style vessels.
The cruise industry projects the luxury cruise fleet will grow from 104 ships in 2026 to at least 130 by 2036.
And while global cruise passengers are forecast to increase about 11% between 2025 and 2028, according to the Cruise Lines International Association, or CLIA, the industry's investment dollars are increasingly flowing to luxury and expedition brands, with orders rising for smaller ships and fewer berths through the next decade.
From 2020 through 2025, there has been a 71% increase in the number of luxury ships and an 84% increase in berths, according to CLIA. The orders indicate another 26% jump in the number of ships through the end of 2029 and a 32% increase in capacity.
Other hospitality companies are investing and expanding their uber-upscale offerings, too. Hotels, casinos, airlines, restaurants, spas and other travel and leisure companies see the trend: Affluent customers remain willing to pay for more space, personal service and distinctive experiences.
United Airlines this year launched its larger Polaris Studio suites with privacy doors and caviar service. American Airlines increased its lie-flat capacity and reported on Thursday that its premium passenger unit revenue rose 18.5% in the third quarter, compared with 14.4% in its main cabin.
Marriott’s second-quarter results showed luxury revenue per available room growing faster than the broader hotel business in the U.S. and Canada, rising 9.1% year over year, compared with 5% for all Marriott hotels in the region. In June, CEO Anthony Capuano told CNBC there's an “almost insatiable appetite” for luxury travel.
Wynn Resorts, which caters almost exclusively to high-end customers, has opted to maintain its high room rates in Las Vegas even as visitation has slowed over the past year, rather than filling every room with travelers looking for a bargain.
Cracks forming in luxury?
Asked onstage at the Global Gaming Expo in September whether Wynn is extracting more profit from fewer customers, CEO Craig Billings said, "We will always focus on what's the most comfortable experience for the customer and where can we drive the highest possible EBITDA."
But offering luxury vacations doesn't guarantee a company's fortunes.
The Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection, for instance, has struggled with occupancy despite major marketing campaigns and has accumulated nearly $700 million in losses since 2017, according to the Financial Times.
More competition in the space could undercut the perception of scarcity. As more companies add luxury products, they have to convince travelers that the experience is worth the price.
Las Vegas Sands reported earnings Wednesday that missed expectations for revenue and profit, hurt by the high-end segments in both Macao and Singapore.
"When the customers don't show up, it's a little problematic," CEO Patrick Dumont said on the company's earnings call.
Dumont said he couldn't point to any single cause for the softness, adding, "perhaps people are not feeling as good about the market," but he said he believes this quarter is an aberration.
CNBC asked Dumont during the NBA China Games in Macao on Oct. 9 whether luxury travel could become overcrowded.
In response, he pointed to the long-term wealth creation in Asia and said the challenge was making experiences customers want to return to.
“It’s not about, are there enough customers,” he said. “It’s, can we create enough of an interesting set of experiences where people find us indispensable in their lives.”

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> American Airlines forecast deeper losses this year and said it will slow its capacity growth next year as record revenue failed to help the airline recover from a spike in fuel costs.
American said Thursday that it expects to lose between $1 and $1.60 a share, on an adjusted basis in 2026. In July, it forecast adjusted full-year earnings per share between a loss of 65 cents and a profit of 65 cents.
The Fort Worth, Texas-based airline is the fourth major U.S. carrier to cut its forecast for 2026 due to a jump in jet fuel following the start of the Iran war. Rising jet fuel prices have driven up airfare more than 20% from last year, according to airline reports and federal data. Delta Air Lines, United Airlines and Southwest Airlines also slashed their outlooks this month.
Jet fuel is airlines' biggest expense after labor, and the spike has added billions to costs this year. American's fuel bill was up 67.5% in the third quarter alone, from last year to $4.64 billion.
"American delivered record third-quarter revenue and the strongest year-over-year revenue growth in my career, with premium performance leading the way," American CEO Robert Isom said in a news release.
Confidence in 2027 profitability
Despite the lower expectations for deeper losses in 2026, Isom said on an earnings call Thursday that he has "great confidence" that the company will return to profitability next year.
The CEO has been under pressure to boost profits at the carrier, which has dramatically trailed profitable rivals Delta and United. American has been investing in new lounges and revamped cabins with more premium seats and said this year that it plans to add seat-back screens again to its narrow-body fleet.
Earlier this month, the airline named a new network chief. It was the latest move in a management shake-up that has included new hires in its operations department and its commercial team. Last year, American brought in a new commercial chief, industry veteran Nat Pieper.
International and premium-travel demand has remained especially robust, executives said. Late last month, American said it would deepen its partnership with Alaska Airlines as the Seattle-based carrier would join American's trans-Atlantic and trans-Pacific joint businesses.
The airline is also closing in on an order for new wide-body airplanes, executives said. CFO Devon May told CNBC on Thursday that the airline has considered the Boeing 787 Dreamliner, Airbus A330 and Airbus A350.
American forecast it will lose between 50 cents and $1.10 a share, adjusted, in the fourth quarter, with revenue expected to be up as much as 22.5% from a year earlier and capacity up between 5.5% and 6.5%.
Here is what American reported in the third quarter compared with Wall Street estimates compiled by LSEG:
- Loss per share: 28 cents adjusted vs. 35 cents expected
- Revenue: $16.34 billion vs. $16.22 billion expected
For the third quarter, American had a net loss of $269 million or 41 cents a share, compared with $114 million, or 17 cents a share, a year earlier. Adjusting for one-time items, American reported a loss of 28 cents a share. Revenue jumped 19.3% from a year earlier to $16.34 billion.

## Procter & Gamble earnings beat as sales rise 4%, boosted by laundry and home products

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/22/procter-gamble-pg-q1-2027-earnings.html

正文长度：3466

摘要：Procter & Gamble topped Wall Street's estimates for its quarterly earnings and revenue.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Procter & Gamble on Thursday reported quarterly earnings and revenue that beat analysts' expectations, fueled by growing demand for its laundry detergent and home care products.
Shares of the company rose more than 5% in morning trading.
Here's what the company reported for the three-month period ended Sept. 30 compared with what Wall Street was expecting, based on a survey of analysts by LSEG:
- Earnings per share: $2.00 adjusted vs. $1.88 expected
- Revenue: $23.3 billion vs. $22.8 billion expected
P&G reported fiscal first-quarter net income attributable to the company of $4.73 billion, or $1.97 per share, down from $4.75 billion, or $1.95 per share, a year earlier.
Excluding restructuring costs and other items, the company earned $2 per share.
Net sales rose 4% to $23.3 billion.
P&G reported volume growth of 2.4%, a strong start for its new fiscal year. Like many consumer companies, P&G has seen demand for its products weaken as shoppers have become more value-conscious.
Executives have previously said that consumers are now using every last drop of shampoo or laundry detergent, and value packs have become more popular. In fiscal 2026, P&G reported just one quarter of volume growth.
P&G is still seeing signs of a bifurcated economy, CFO Andre Schulten said on CNBC's "Squawk Box" on Thursday. High-income consumers are willing to trade up to new products, even a $30 shampoo, while low-income consumers are trying to make brands like Tide and Pampers work for their tight budgets.
The company has tried to address both ends of the spectrum with promotional pricing, new pack sizes and product innovation that can command higher prices. New premium products like Pampers Amore and Pantene's Abundant & Strong haircare line have helped P&G grow market share in those categories, executives said.
Adding to P&G's challenges, the competitive environment is "intense," Schulten said on the company's quarterly earnings call.
"We are getting the balance between pricing and product improvements, package improvements, claims, communication and retail execution right," Schulten told analysts. "But it's also a very fragile balance that we want to make sure we we don't compromise on, so we will price, and we will price with innovation, not jeopardizing the top-line momentum."
P&G's top-performing division was fabric and home care, which includes brands like Tide and Febreze. The segment's volume rose 4.3% in the quarter, helped by stronger demand for laundry detergent and fabric enhancers.
The company's baby, feminine and family care segment and its beauty business both reported volume growth of more than 2%.
P&G's grooming division saw volume shrink 0.4% in the quarter. The segment includes Gillette and Venus razors.
The company's healthcare business was the laggard of the portfolio this quarter. Its volume fell 2.9% as demand for oral healthcare products, like Crest toothpaste, and personal healthcare items, like Pepto-Bismol, shrank.
The company is expecting continued "geopolitical and economic challenges," but P&G is on track to meet its guidance, CEO Shailesh Jejurikar said in a statement.
For fiscal 2027, P&G reiterated its forecast for core earnings per share in a range of $6.89 to $7.11. The company is now projecting all-in sales growth in the range of 2% to 4% compared with the prior year, up from its previous outlook of 1% to 3%. The change reflects its acquisition of supplements brand Thorne, which closed Oct. 1.
