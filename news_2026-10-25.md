## Marc Lore’s Wonder bets on Texas, automation and user-built restaurants as it mulls IPO

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/25/wonder-ipo-texas-automation.html

正文长度：10502

摘要：Wonder is leaning into automation and user-built restaurants as founder Marc Lore prepares the startup for a potential IPO.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Less than a decade after its founding, food startup Wonder has seen its footprint and valuation soar.
Still, the company has even more ambitious plans ahead, like a subscription program to let diners run their own "restaurants," an aggressive expansion in Texas and a potential initial public offering.
Created by serial entrepreneur Marc Lore in 2018, Wonder advertises itself as "the best restaurants from across the country, all in one place." Diners can place takeout or delivery orders from its food hall-style locations on the Wonder-branded app or third-party platforms, including Grubhub, which Wonder now owns.
As Lore described it to CNBC, "Wonder is a vertically integrated food technology platform to make great food more accessible."
To achieve its goal, Wonder plans to open locations in urban, suburban and rural areas nationwide and aims to keep prices lower than the cost of delivered groceries. Thanks to its ownership of Grubhub, Wonder customers who order through the startup's app don't have to pay delivery fees, and its menu prices aren't inflated to cover the cost of commission fees to the delivery platform. Plus, Wonder is automating some of its kitchen functions, like assembling salads and bowls and soon sauces and beverages.
The company aims for food preparation and delivery to be fully autonomous by 2040, Lore said.
Now, Wonder has to convince diners its platform built on cost savings and choice makes it worth their while in a competitive industry.
Lore's lofty vision for Wonder has helped the company raise $3.3 billion to date, from investors like DoorDash, Alphabet's venture arm and Gwyneth Paltrow. Its Series D fundraising round earlier this year raised its pre-money valuation to $9 billion — just under sandwich chain Subway's sale price two years ago.
Wonder's next pitch to investors could be on an IPO road show, assuming that the window for companies across industries to go public reopens in the coming months.
"[The mission] is really why I want to go public and raise the capital that it'll take to build that kind of generational business, also to have a public currency to be able to do more M&A," Lore said. "But I can't talk about timing."
Lore's track record
Lore's expertise lies in e-commerce, not restaurants.
He helped found the parent company of Diapers.com and sold it in 2010 to Amazon for $545 million. Just six years later, Walmart paid about $3 billion for his follow-up venture, Jet.com. Lore stayed at the retailer until 2021 to accelerate Walmart's e-commerce growth.
Basketball fans might also recognize Lore as an owner of the Minnesota Timberwolves. In 2021, he and baseball legend Alex Rodriguez agreed to buy the team for $1.5 billion, although the deal was not finalized until 2025. Lore recently struck a deal to shrink his investment to a minority stake "to focus on his business," Rodriguez said in a statement in August, referring to Wonder.
Lore's latest startup began with the idea of bringing food delivery to consumers' homes through sprinter vans outfitted with mobile kitchens. The trucks lasted roughly two years before Wonder phased them out in favor of brick-and-mortar locations, which could serve more food options and lower costs.
Lore's successful track record has helped him win over investors for Wonder.
"I joke around at GV that I do enterprise software investing, AI investing and anything that Marc Lore is running as an active CEO," said Dave Munichiello, managing partner of Alphabet's venture capital arm GV, who has known Lore since his Diapers.com days and invested in Jet.com through GV.
Lore himself thinks that his vision is the reason for Wonder's billions of dollars of funding.
"Everybody eats," he said. "It's defensible from AI."
Wonder has spent more than $1 billion on acquisitions, not including the deals where financial details were not disclosed. Its purchases of Grubhub, delivery and logistics provider Relay, meal-kit provider Blue Apron and buzzy New York restaurant concepts have been expensive.
But Lore said he sees a "tremendous total addressable market" for Wonder if it's able to sell high-quality food at accessible prices, particularly in exurban and rural areas with few dining options beyond fast food and gas stations.
Wonder gets bigger in Texas
From Virginia to Massachusetts, 170 Wonder storefronts have appeared over the last three years. Next year, the company plans to enter Texas for the first time — with a goal of at least 100 locations in the state by the end of 2027.
Wonder prepares its food at commissary kitchens, then transports it to each individual location, where employees unseal, cook and finish the menu items. Its storefronts are all electric, without gas or venting, so Wonder can quickly move into a new space and cut down on renovations and red tape.
Its locations serve menu items from more than two dozen "restaurants," including Dabba Indian Kitchen, Tejas Barbecue and Pop Salad. Celebrity chefs including Bobby Flay, Michael Symon and Marcus Samuelsson receive up-front payments and equity in Wonder to let the company use their names for some of those concepts. More recent additions include acquisitions such as Blue Ribbon Fried Chicken and Salt Hank's, brands with established followings.
With its wide variety of options housed under one roof, Wonder has drawn comparisons to ghost kitchens, the once-buzzy startups without dine-in seating that flamed out as food delivery orders fell after the Covid pandemic. Once considered a potential disruptor to the restaurant industry, ghost kitchen companies such as Kitchen United have sought bankruptcy protection and wound down their operations.
Lore disagrees with the comparison.
"You can see there's a front of house [in the] brick and mortar," he said. "You'll see people at lunchtime, sitting there enjoying lunch, so it's not ghost-y in that way. But I think it's also just in the fact that we're vertically integrated."
Wonder has never marketed itself as a ghost kitchen, instead calling itself "a new kind of food hall" or a "modern food court" before landing on a "vertically integrated food technology platform."
Starting Nov. 17, the company plans to take the next step with Wonder Create. Influencers, entrepreneurs and brands can pay $10 a month to start their own "restaurant" based out of a Wonder location and earn a portion of the revenue from orders. Artificial intelligence will help them make their brand, including name, logo, pricing and recipes.
"It could be a restaurant for you and your friends, or you could be an influencer that has a following," Lore said.
He wants Wonder Create to democratize restaurant ownership — minus the capital, overhead costs or oversight of kitchen operations. The platform will eventually be available to the public, with users deciding menu items based on Wonder's pantry.
Besides Wonder Create, the company is also using AI for another initiative. MEL, a proprietary AI platform, can use a customer's health information, like data from an Oura ring or Whoop tracker or even blood biomarkers, to prepare breakfast, lunch and dinner for its user.
MEL is not currently available to Wonder customers, although Lore uses AI to advise his own meals, like the chicken muffin he ate for breakfast on a Tuesday in October.
The quality question
While Wonder has won over venture capitalists, the battle for diners' stomachs and wallets is tougher.
Budget-conscious consumers have been dining out less often and becoming pickier when they do so. And while food delivery is still more popular than it was before the pandemic, consumers have pulled back on delivery orders compared with their Covid-era peak. Fees and high menu prices, which offset eateries' commission fees with apps, make delivery even more expensive than dining at a restaurant.
Plus, some consumers are skeptical about Wonder more specifically. Posts on social media decry the company's food quality and its funding from venture capital.
"It's OK, but it's just average," Amir Ings, a 27-year-old living in Philadelphia, said in a TikTok reviewing an order of three tacos from Wonder in late 2025. "My expectations were already in the gutter — it's not as bad as I thought it was going to be, but it's still not great. And I paid like $16 for this, but it should have been no more than $8."
Lore told CNBC that Wonder receives thousands of reviews every day from customers, rating the taste, temperature and portion size of their orders on a scale from 1 to 10. Wonder starts its board meetings by talking about taste scores, Munichiello said.
"We're maniacally focused on food quality .... We're constantly iterating on the meals that score lower, and see over time, the last 12 months, our taste scores have improved, our Google reviews have improved," Lore said. "I do expect food quality to improve as we scale, which is not always the case."
Despite lackluster reviews on social media, Wonder has fans. Based on transaction data from Indagari, Wonder had a higher weighted average retention rate — how likely a diner is to return — and higher lifetime value — how much a customer has spent over time — than Cava and Sweetgreen.
Plus, Wonder has succeeded with locations in the most competitive markets for restaurants in the country.
"They made this company work well in the hardest place in the country to stand up this model: New York," Munichiello said. "You have so much choice in New York."
Ultimately, Wonder wants to win over the rest of the U.S. — and public market investors.
In March, the company hired Gabrielle Scheibe Rabinovitch, previously at payment processor Worldpay, as its chief financial officer as it prepares to go public.
Lore declined to comment on the timing of a potential IPO to CNBC, although he has previously told Fortune that the company will be "ready and prepared to go public early next year."
Since buying Blue Apron and Grubhub, Wonder has been operating at a financial loss, The Information previously reported. It plans to bring down the price of its meals by leaning into automation and drone delivery. In the long term, that plan could cut costs, although laying the groundwork won't be cheap.
In recent years, investors have grown less tolerant of startups pursuing growth at all costs, according to Avery Marquez, director of investment strategies at Renaissance Capital. To sway investors, unprofitable companies have to show they are taking steps toward profitability — or that the growth will continue long after the IPO.

## Why popular airplanes are getting scrapped years early

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/24/spirit-airlines-planes-scrapped-engine-shortage.html

正文长度：7657

摘要：Airplanes are getting dismantled for parts years ahead of schedule because engines and other components are in short supply.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> GOODYEAR, Ariz. — An Airbus A320neo, one of the world's most popular planes, rolled off the Mobile, Alabama, factory line in July 2022, painted bright yellow for its next destination: Spirit Airlines.
It was built to fly for decades. But this May, less than four years later, it was stripped for parts worth millions and crushed, the steel teeth of an excavator tearing through its fuselage at a facility on the outskirts of Phoenix.
More aircraft are getting dismantled years, if not decades, earlier than expected, industry executives told CNBC. It is the result of a shortage of parts, none more precious and expensive than aircraft engines, that has exasperated airlines and driven up costs for years.
Even after the engines are removed, firms are looking for parts as varied as landing gear and antennae to sell.
"It's the first time in my career that involved purchasing an aircraft that's less than five years since date of manufacture," said Bill Thompson, vice president who leads aircraft and engine acquisition for the Americas at aviation asset management and trading firm EirTrade Aviation, which bought the less than 4-year-old Airbus A320neo earlier this year.
He said the company paid "single-digit" millions each for that airplane and a similarly aged one, and it expects to make a profit from selling the more than 1,000 parts it took off them before they were crushed.
"I don't know if you're going to sell the coffee makers but you never know. Sometimes you do," he said.
'Don't fall in love with the metal'
Twenty-one newest-generation aircraft were torn down from June 2025 through this past August, according to aviation data and advisory firm IBA Group. The scrapped planes were 6.4 years old on average, and included mostly Airbus but also some Boeing planes. That compares with an average retirement age for narrow-body airplanes of 21.7 years, IBA said.
"Anytime that you end up with any period of market softness in aircraft availability, engine availability, it's going to cause investors to look at other options," said Simon Goodson, CEO of AerFin, a company that buys, sells, repairs and leases aircraft and parts. "We always say to our aircraft and engine traders, 'Please don't fall in love with the metal.'"
Hundreds of aircraft are retired each year as manufacturers ramp up production for airlines, so older planes are swapped out for newer, more fuel-efficient models.
But aside from retiring aging planes, some owners don't want to wait around for replacement parts or repairs on younger jets.
"A year might not sound that long, but does three or four years?" said Dave Querio, CEO of Ascent Aviation Services, a company that repairs, modifies, stores and disassembles aircraft, based in Marana, Arizona, outside of Tucson. "That's the issue for the aircraft that came in in '23, '24 and '25."
In some cases, owners can reap more for the parts than they can selling the plane or paying storage costs and waiting for it to get back in the air again.
Querio said he hasn't seen "the volume of younger aircraft being torn down as I'm seeing today."
Hot engine market
A major reason for the young retirements is the long wait and rising prices for jet engines.
One problem is that cutting-edge engines that burn hotter and use less fuel are coming up for maintenance far earlier than airlines expected.
The maintenance requests have kept engine repair and overhaul facilities backed up, from those owned by manufacturing giants GE Aerospace and RTX unit Pratt & Whitney to smaller, independent ones. Airlines and leasing firms have been hunting for spare parts and engines anywhere they can find them, like here in Goodyear, Arizona.
The trend has added more fuel to the already hot secondhand parts market. An engine fan blade could cost $50,000 or even more than $100,000 for bigger jets, Goodson said. Engines that power some Airbus A320neo aircraft have 20 of those blades. A disc for the engine, meanwhile, could cost between $1 million and $2 million.
An engine recall because of a manufacturing defect has backed up repair shops and grounded hundreds of Airbus planes around the world. Spirit Airlines bore the brunt of that problem in the United States, years before the airline collapsed.
The engine is made by Pratt & Whitney, which issued the recall in 2023 after discovering contaminated powdered metal used in making some parts. Those engines are worth close to $17 million this year at the high end, up 60% from the upper end of the range in 2019, according to IBA.
Airlines are removing some engines off retired planes to keep current aircraft flying while they wait for engines to come back from the shop.
"So you're just playing Legos and moving engines on and off aircraft," said Mike Stengel, a partner at AeroDynamic Advisory, an aerospace consulting firm.
The problems are driving up the cost to buy or rent one of the popular engines to record levels.
Renting one is ranging from about $160,000 to $180,000 a month, according to IBA data. A competing engine for the A320neo family made by CFM International, a joint venture of GE Aerospace and France's Safran, is far from a bargain, renting in a similar range of $130,000 to $180,000 a month.
Spirit Airlines' ghost planes
The problems with young retirements predate Spirit's collapse.
The 4-year-old Airbus plane that was torn down outside Phoenix in May is a key example. The aircraft was put in storage because of the engine recall, and it stayed until it was torn apart.
But the company's demise has brought more of its Airbus fleet to the market, forcing owners to figure out if it's worth the wait for engines or other parts.
CNBC visited AerSale's facility in Goodyear on a scorching day in late June, less than two months after Spirit collapsed, where dozens of the idle yellow planes were stored. It was so soon after the airline's demise that a former Detroit-based Spirit captain, Glenn Zecchin, popped in and asked about opportunities to ferry Spirit's aircraft to the desert, and to peek in on his former airline's planes.
"I knew the airplanes were there, and I wanted to see what happened," said Zecchin, who is now flying corporate jets.
A jet parked at the storage facility and maintenance hangar still had Spirit in-flight menus and safety cards in the bungee cord seatbacks. Pilot manuals were still in the cockpit.
Not all of these aircraft will be destroyed. Some companies that owned Spirit's planes before bankruptcy will just sell their parts, but some may keep the planes in storage. Other aircraft may go on to fly for other airlines, with potential carriers ripping out Spirit's tight 28-inch seat pitch for something roomier.
But as long as they're not flying, it means paying storage fees, which vary by aircraft type. Some planes in desert facilities — selected because the arid climate can preserve the aircraft better and avoid corrosion — can sit for decades.
Others won't make it back into the air if there's enough demand for the parts and too long of a wait to get the plane flying again.
"After you get rid of all the valuable equipment and sell it back into the aftermarket, what's really left is just a metal carcass that you will literally take a crane and crush," said AeroDynamic's Stengel. Then the owner can "sell it to recyclers that will make of it what they will, maybe into beer cans or something outside of aerospace."
Ascent's Querio said some owners opt to take that route.
"It's a little melancholy to watch it at times," he said. "But sometimes, it's kind of a thrill to ... see how fast an aircraft can be crunched up and sent off to the smelter."
— CNBC's Erin Black contributed to this report.

## Restaurant earnings will test whether owning more brands pays off

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/darden-brinker-qsr-stocks-restaurant-earnings.html

正文长度：3927

摘要：Shares of companies with multiple restaurant brands like Brinker and Darden are outperforming single-chain stocks like McDonald's and Chipotle.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Restaurant stocks have split sharply this year. And it could be tied to how many brands they own.
Shares of Brinker International, Darden Restaurants and Restaurant Brands International are up roughly 32%, 18% and 12%, respectively, year to date. Those companies all own more than one restaurant chain, with brands collectively ranging from Chili's to Olive Garden, LongHorn Steakhouse, Burger King and Tim Hortons.
Meanwhile, stocks like McDonald's, Chipotle, Cava, Wendy's, Shake Shack and Domino's have all dropped this year, with several down double-digit percentages. Those companies all have one core chain to their names.
Those share moves underscore what some analysts see as the benefits of a diversified restaurant business, especially at a time when any misstep can make cash-strapped consumers go elsewhere when they dine out.
Investors will get another look at how operating multiple chains — or not — is affecting performance when major restaurant companies report quarterly results starting next week. One company that could be watching is Inspire Brands, the owner of chains like Dunkin', Buffalo Wild Wings and Jimmy John's, which is moving toward going public.
A business built around one main restaurant concept depends more heavily on that brand holding consumers' attention, whereas a company with a portfolio has other potential sources of sales if one brand loses momentum.
That cushion can be critical, but only if another chain is strong enough to pick up the slack, according to some Wall Street analysts.
"Darden is probably the best case of the scale and size and diversity of business," said Nick Setyan, a restaurant analyst at Mizuho Securities. "If one business isn't doing well, the other ones are."
Setyan said that dynamic has played out between Darden's key brands — Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse — over recent quarters. LongHorn's growth has outpaced Olive Garden's for months, and in the latest reported quarter, LongHorn's comparable sales rose 6.2% compared with Olive Garden's 1.1%.
KeyBanc Capital Markets also cited the range of Darden's portfolio as a positive in an October research note, saying it gives the company "exposure to a broad range of consumers." The company's fine dining and casual offerings can be beneficial in a K-shaped economy where consumer spending can vary by income.
The firm has a buy-equivalent rating on Darden and a $237 price target, compared with a price of around $215 as of Thursday.
Restaurant Brands International, similarly, offers exposure to different categories and markets through Burger King, Tim Hortons and Popeyes. But its portfolio does not fully insulate the company from a weak brand.
In the second quarter, Burger King's U.S. comparable sales accelerated to 8.5%, from 5.8% in the first quarter, while Tim Hortons' Canadian comparable sales slowed to just 0.1%, from 1.5% over the same period. That left Burger King's turnaround carrying more of the portfolio as Tim Hortons stalled.
Setyan said Burger King's recent strong performance has been central to the company's momentum, helped by its ability to better communicate its value items to customers as McDonald's sales growth slowed.
"If it weren't for Burger King's current performance, I don't know if [Restaurant Brands] would be going so well," he said.
Mizuho initiated coverage of Restaurant Brands on Oct. 20 with a buy-equivalent rating and an $85 price target. Shares traded at about $76 as of Thursday.
A focus on one brand can also be a strength. Starbucks and Cheesecake Factory have been restaurant-stock standouts this year, showing how a clear brand identity and investment can drive outperformance when execution and customer demand click. Their shares have climbed 21% and nearly 123%, respectively, this year.
And Starbucks, for its part, said it would not buy Chipotle after reportedly considering a massive buyout that would have diversified its business.

## Fewer people are flying as airfare soars. Here's why airlines aren't worried

来源：CNBC

链接：https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/airfare-airline-capacity.html

正文长度：6126

摘要：Airlines are reporting fewer people flew in the third quarter as airfare surged on fuel costs, but executives say travelers are mostly swallowing higher fares.

<details>
<summary><b>👉 点击展开 / 收起正文</b></summary>

> Fewer people are flying this year, but with airfare this high and climbing, airline executives say that's just fine.
At the New York Hilton in Midtown Manhattan on Friday night, the aviation industry held its annual Wings Club gala, honoring Ed Bastian, CEO of profit-leader Delta Air Lines.
North American airline CEOs and other executives at the black-tie bash told CNBC that demand trends aren't worrying them, but persistently high, profit-crimping fuel prices are.
The number of people screened at U.S. checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Major U.S. carriers have said capturing higher fares matters more than fully filling planes or flying record numbers of travelers.
"We're actively prioritizing yield versus load factor and really happy as we see it. It fits right in with the premium investments that we're making," American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said on the company's earnings call Thursday.
American's load factor, a gauge of how full planes are flying, fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% in the same quarter of 2025.
Fares keep climbing
Plane ticket prices have skyrocketed as airlines grapple with a spike in jet fuel prices, their biggest expense after labor, and pass more of those costs on to customers.
Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation read. Fare tracker Hopper said it expects this holiday season to come with the most expensive ticket prices in years.
The average Southwest Airlines fare in the third quarter, for example, was $231.69, up close to $50 from a year earlier. That doesn't include Southwest's new fees, like its extra charges for checked bags or a seat closer to the front of the plane.
The airline's revenue passenger count, however, fell 3.2% from a year ago to about 33.5 million people.
Still, both fares and the passenger counts exceeded Southwest's expectations.
"Passenger fares came in about $3 above what our forecast was and the more important thing is that passenger volumes also exceeded our expectations," Southwest CEO Bob Jordan said on an earnings call Thursday.
Since April — when airlines began warning in earnest about profits taking a hit from the spike in jet fuel — through Thursday, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened at airport checkpoints than in the same period of 2025, according to the Transportation Security Administration. That's down less than 2% from last year, but it follows years of growth.
With jet fuel prices more than double year-ago levels — as seen in U.S. Gulf Coast spot prices at $4.63 a gallon, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — airlines have focused on recovering as much of the fuel spike as possible.
Despite some drops, airline executives said during their earnings calls that overall travel demand is robust, especially for profitable premium cabins like business class.
And not every carrier is seeing a decline. United Airlines flew 2.4% more passengers in the third quarter than a year ago and said its unit revenue rose 16%, the biggest increase since the pandemic.
"I've been pleasantly surprised at the industry's ability this year to raise yields and pass through much of the increase in fuel prices," CEO Scott Kirby said on the company's earnings call Wednesday. "So it's possible that airlines will continue to focus on yields at the expense of load factor and that alone could get us to double-digit margins next year."
Profit hit
Most airlines expect fuel costs to keep biting, but executives say they're confident in their pricing power.
United, for example, said it expects to pay an average of $4.80 per gallon for fuel in the fourth quarter, up from $3.90 per gallon in the third.
American, United, Delta and Southwest all cut their 2026 profit forecasts this month when they reported earnings. American is the only one of the four that expects to post a loss this year.
American and others have also reduced growth plans or warned the industry could dial back its capacity expansion goals, which could keep fares elevated. Reduced capacity can make bookable seats scarcer, driving up prices even more if demand holds.
The carriers said demand has been particularly strong for premium seats, which command hundreds, if not thousands of dollars, more than coach tickets.
"Today, we find ourselves yet at another inflection point for our industry and the world. ... Geopolitics affecting our business, driving the price of jet fuel to historic highs," Bastian said in his award-acceptance speech, adding that artificial intelligence makes it hard to predict months or weeks ahead.
"It causes many people to question yet again the durability of our industry. But I'm not worried because I know the people in this room, and I know your resilience and how you've demonstrated that time and time again," he said.
Smaller airlines set to report results in the coming weeks, starting with JetBlue Airways next week, followed by Frontier Airlines and Allegiant Air, have traditionally focused on more price-sensitive customers, which could show if those travelers are pulling back at all.
One factor that could also affect air travel is the demise of Spirit Airlines, which went out of business in early May.
"Some of its customers have likely stopped flying," Bank of America said in a research note Thursday.
More jets, engine hangover
Many of the problems that plagued the airline industry for years are starting to abate.
In some cases, aircraft deliveries are still years behind schedule. But Boeing, whose CEO, Kelly Ortberg, attended on Friday night, and its main rival, Airbus, have ramped up production. Boeing will update investors on its production outlook when it reports quarterly results Tuesday.
The Federal Aviation Administration has approved Boeing's Max 7, a key smaller jet for Southwest Airlines, and the Max 10, which Alaska Airlines and United Airlines have been waiting for for years.
Pratt & Whitney parent, RTX, and GE Aerospace say they're improving engine durability and shop visit turnaround time, though customers say more improvements are needed.
